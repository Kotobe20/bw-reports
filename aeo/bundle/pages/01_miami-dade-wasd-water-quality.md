**Short answer:** Yes, Miami-Dade tap water met every federal and state limit in 2025, per the county's own report. The same report lists PFOS ("forever chemicals") at up to 33 parts per trillion against a 4 ppt federal limit that is not enforced until 2029, and chloramine as high as 4.0 ppm. Legal and empty are not the same thing.

## The 2025 numbers, straight from the report

Main System (Alexander Orr, Hialeah and John E. Preston plants). Highest level reported unless noted.

| What was measured | Main System result | Federal limit | Violation? |
|---|---|---|---|
| PFOS (a PFAS "forever chemical") | 33 ppt (range 7 to 33) | 4 ppt, enforced from 2029 | Not reported until 2029 |
| PFOA (a PFAS) | 13 ppt (range 3 to 13) | 4 ppt, enforced from 2029 | Not reported until 2029 |
| PFHxS (a PFAS) | 7 ppt (range 2 to 7) | 10 ppt | Not reported until 2029 |
| Chloramine (disinfectant) | 2.5 ppm average (not detected to 4.0) | 4.0 ppm maximum | No |
| Total trihalomethanes (disinfection by-products) | 50 ppb average (range 9 to 59) | 80 ppb | No |
| Haloacetic acids (disinfection by-products) | 34 ppb average (range 10 to 43) | 60 ppb | No |
| Lead at the tap (90th percentile) | 3.2 ppb; 1 of 102 homes over the action level | 15 ppb action level | No |
| Fluoride | No longer added since June 6, 2025; natural 0.1 to 0.3 ppm | 4 ppm | No |

South Dade system (south of SW 264 Street, Homestead area): PFOS 32 ppt, PFOA 13 ppt, nitrate 7 ppm against a 10 ppm limit.

<small>Source: Miami-Dade Water and Sewer Department, 2025 Water Quality Report (Consumer Confidence Report). ppt = parts per trillion. ppb = parts per billion. ppm = parts per million. The report says PFAS violations will not be reported until 2029. In May 2026 the EPA proposed keeping the 4 ppt PFOA and PFOS limits while letting utilities ask for an extension to 2031; check epa.gov for the current status.</small>

## What the report does not tell you

1. **Your pipes.** The utility tests water leaving the plant and at a sample of homes. It cannot test the pipes inside your house or condo building. Lead usually comes from plumbing, not the aquifer.
2. **Hardness.** The report does not list a hardness number for the water that reaches you. The only way to know is to test at your tap.
3. **Health guidelines that are stricter than the law.** The Environmental Working Group (EWG), a nonprofit, lists 18 contaminants in Miami-Dade water above its own health guidelines (2026 data set). EWG's guidelines are not legal limits, and the utility was in compliance with federal standards.

<small>Source: EWG Tap Water Database, Miami Dade Water and Sewer Authority, 2026 data set, ewg.org/tapwater. EWG health guidelines are not enforceable standards.</small>

## Where the forever chemicals are highest

Researchers at Florida International University tested about 72 tap water samples across 18 Miami-Dade ZIP codes over five years. They found 80 to 95 percent of samples were above the new 4 ppt limits. The highest levels were in Little Havana, Hialeah, Grapeland Heights near the airport, Homestead and Pembroke Pines.

<small>Source: Miami Herald, "Miami-Dade has forever chemicals in tap water. There's a plan to keep it drinkable," Aug 7, 2026, reporting FIU research by Natalia Soares Quinete and Carolina Cuchimaque Lugo.</small>

Miami-Dade is piloting carbon, ion exchange and reverse osmosis treatment at its plants. The county's position is that tap water meets every current rule and residents do not need a filter. Many families filter anyway, for taste, for smell, or because they would rather not wait until 2029.

## What actually removes each one

| Concern | What reduces it | What does not |
|---|---|---|
| Chloramine taste and smell | Catalytic carbon, sized for the whole house | Most pitcher filters, a basic softener |
| PFAS (PFOS, PFOA) | Reverse osmosis at the tap; certified PFAS media (look for NSF/ANSI 53 or 58 PFOA/PFOS claims) | A water softener alone |
| Disinfection by-products | Carbon filtration | A softener alone |
| Lead from home plumbing | Point-of-use filter certified for lead (NSF/ANSI 53 or 58) | Boiling water |
| Hardness and scale | A salt-based softener | A carbon filter alone |

<small>Treatment guidance reflects EWG filtering options for this utility and NSF/ANSI certification standards. Always check the exact certification of the exact model before you buy.</small>

## Miami-Dade tap water: common questions

**Is Miami tap water safe to drink?**
It meets every current federal and state limit, per WASD's 2025 report. It also contains PFOS and PFOA above the 4 ppt limits that take effect in 2029, plus chloramine and disinfection by-products below their limits. Whether to filter is a personal call; a home test shows what is in your own tap.

**Does Miami-Dade water have PFAS?**
Yes. The 2025 report lists PFOS up to 33 ppt and PFOA up to 13 ppt in the Main System, and similar levels in South Dade. The federal limit is 4 ppt each, enforced from 2029.

**Does Miami-Dade still add fluoride?**
No. The Orr, Preston and Hialeah plants stopped adding fluoride on June 6, 2025. Natural levels now run 0.1 to 0.3 ppm, according to the 2025 report.

**Is Miami water chlorine or chloramine?**
The Main System uses chloramine (chlorine plus ammonia), averaging 2.5 ppm in 2025. The South Dade system uses free chlorine. Chloramine needs catalytic carbon to remove well.

**How hard is Miami-Dade water?**
WASD's 2025 report does not publish a hardness number for delivered water. Test at your tap before you buy a softener.

**Do I need a filter in Miami?**
WASD says no filter is needed to meet current standards. Families who want lower PFAS, less chloramine taste, or no scale usually filter. A free in-home test tells you which problems your water actually has.
