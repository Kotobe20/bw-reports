Gary Halbert & David Ogilvy’s copywriting brain

All the principles and playbooks, in one printable file. Short words. Real sources. No made-up quotes.

How to read this. Quoted lines were confirmed in a source in section 9. Otherwise I paraphrase and say so. Do not invent proof, quotes, deadlines, or “reasons why.” BL = The Boron Letters. GHL = Halbert’s newsletter. HCATS = Ogilvy’s 38-point ad. OoA/Conf = his books (chapter notes). Memo 1982 = “How to Write.”

1. One-page cheat sheet

Halbert, in plain words
  1. Find a starving crowd first. Then write. (BL Ch. 6; GHL “The One Advantage,” Oct. 1, 1986)
  2. Look like a person, not an ad. Get into the A-pile. (BL Ch. 10–11, 18)
  3. Write in order: Attention, Interest, Desire, Action. (BL Ch. 15–16)
  4. Headlines: news and a benefit, with exact details. Customize to the reader. (GHL “killer headlines”; BL Ch. 9–10)
  5. The deal is the heart of the copy. Give a true reason why the deal exists. (GHL “best copywriter”; BL Ch. 21)
  6. Close hard. Lead them by the hand. Up to 25% of the ad can be the close. (BL Ch. 16)
  7. Plain words. Short lines. Read it out loud and fix every stumble. (BL Ch. 17, 22)
  8. Test small (1,000–5,000), then roll out winners. (BL Ch. 22)
Ogilvy, in plain words
  1. We sell, or else. Judge by sales, not awards. (Conf notes; HCATS #8; OoA Ch. 1 notes)
  2. Position first: what it is, and who it is for. (HCATS #1)
  3. One large, unique promise — a benefit, not a slogan. (HCATS #2, #11)
  4. You need a big idea that could run 30 years. (HCATS #4; OoA Ch. 2 notes)
  5. About 5 times as many people read the headline as the body. Put the brand and the promise in it. (HCATS #25; OoA Ch. 7 notes)
  6. Give facts, not adjectives. “The consumer isn’t a moron; she is your wife.” (HCATS #13; Conf notes)
  7. Long copy works if it is true and interesting. “The more you tell, the more you sell.” (HCATS #32)
  8. Repeat winners. Test promises. Do your homework. (HCATS #38; OoA Ch. 2, 12 notes)
Start any ad, letter, page, or script with this
  1. Name the hungry crowd. (Halbert)
  2. Decide the position and the one promise. (Ogilvy)
  3. Build a Fact Sheet, a Benefit List, and a Deal Sheet. (GHL “best copywriter”)
  4. Write 16 or more headlines. Pick the one that sells alone. (Conf Ch. 6 excerpt)
  1. Tell a true story with exact details. Picture the benefit in their life.
  2. State the deal, the true reason why, and the risk-reversal.
  3. Tell them exactly what to do next. Then say it again.
  4. Read it out loud. Cut jargon. Check every quote and number.

2. The Halbert brain

Gary C. Halbert (1938–2007) was a direct-response copywriter. He taught in letters to his son Bond, later published as The Boron Letters (written June–July 1984 from the Federal Prison Camp at Boron, California), and in his newsletter, The Gary Halbert Letter. The points below come from those sources, not from later commentators.

Core beliefs

1. Find the starving crowd first

Halbert asked students: if you both ran a hamburger stand, what advantage would you want? After they named better meat or better buns, he said he would take only one advantage:

“The only advantage I want,” I reply, “is A STARVING CROWD!” — BL Ch. 6 (June 17, 1984). He told the same story again in GHL “The One Advantage,” Oct. 1, 1986.

Pick people who have already shown they want the result — by buying a like product, joining a list, or asking for help — before you write a word. Find the market first. Then find or make the product. (BL Ch. 6, 22; GHL “The One Advantage”)

2. Get into the A-pile

Halbert said everyone splits their mail into two piles. The A-pile looks personal (friends, family, work). The B-pile looks like a sales pitch. Everyone opens the A-pile. The B-pile may be tossed unopened. First job: make the envelope look personal — a live stamp, a typed or handwritten address, no company name, no “teaser” copy. (BL Ch. 10 preview and Ch. 11, which sits in the same letter file after June 21.)

YOU CAN DO A BETTER SELLING JOB WHEN AT FIRST IT DOES NOT APPEAR YOU ARE ATTEMPTING TO DO A SALES JOB. — BL Ch. 18, his capitals.

For ads and pages today, the same idea holds: write like a person. Use a native or editorial look. Do not use fake “teaser” hype.

3. The sale happens at first glance

I believe the “sale” or “no sale” decision is largely made the instant a prospect sees your ad and reads your headline. — BL Ch. 19

If the look and the headline give an instant lift, the reader starts hunting for reasons to believe you. Do not disappoint them.

4. Write for money, not applause

The very best writing goes unnoticed. — BL Ch. 17
If you are writing for applause... you will go home with empty pockets! — BL Ch. 17

You do not want “what a well-written ad.” You want an order.

Headline formulas

In GHL “killer headlines,” Halbert says the strongest headlines put in news, promise a benefit, or both. Specifics make them believable. He uses “In Just 17-Days” as an example: the exact number feels more real than a vague “soon.” He also wrote:

I, myself, have re-headlined ads and increased their pull by 475%. — GHL “killer headlines.” This is his own claim about work he did; I do not have the original test reports.

Customize the headline to the reader. From BL Ch. 9:

  • Weak: “How To Buy Real Estate With No Money Down”
  • Stronger: “How To Buy L.A. Real Estate With No Money Down”
  • Even tighter, in a letter: “Dear Doctor X, Here’s a unique way L.A. chiropractors can purchase California real estate…”

He said he wrote an ad like that, customized it, and it worked much better. (BL Ch. 9)

Attention must be honest. A grabber has to tie in with the message. He repeats a bad example from an old Printers’ Ink writer: the headline “A Submarine That Flies?” followed by “No, we don’t have a submarine that flies but our pink pills…” His verdict:

A cheap shot. People resent this kind of fraud. Don’t do it. — BL Ch. 14

Letter structure: AIDA, on purpose

BL Ch. 15–16 and GHL “AIDA” use the old formula: Attention, Interest, Desire, Action. Write each part on purpose.

  1. Attention. Get it, or nothing else gets read. Use a relevant grabber, not a bait headline. (BL Ch. 14, 16)
  2. Interest. Feed interesting facts. Tell a story. In GHL “AIDA” he asks what is most often missing in a sales message, and answers: “the sales message doesn’t tell an interesting story.” (GHL “AIDA”; also GHL “interesting facts”)
  3. Desire. Turn facts into benefits. A fact is a fact about the product. A benefit is what it will do for the buyer. (GHL “best copywriter”) Use word pictures so the reader can feel the result. “Remember, you must always do even the obvious.” (BL Ch. 16) Picture the new car, the quiet house, the peace of mind — even when it feels obvious.
  4. Action. Be clear and specific. “Lead him by the hand and take him exactly where you want him to go.” Tell them where the coupon is, what to fill out, how much to send, who to make the check out to, whether the envelope needs a stamp. Say what they get if they hurry and what they lose if they wait. “Sometimes I devote as 25% or more of the entire ad to closing.” (BL Ch. 16; wording as printed, including “as 25%.”)

The offer, the reason why, and risk reversal

It Is The Deal... The Offer... The Proposition You Are Making That Is The Heart And Soul Of Great Copywriting! — GHL “best copywriter” (his capitals)

He says writing “copy” is “less than 1/10 as important” as thinking up a stronger offer and getting it on paper. A strong offer can save weak copy. Strong copy will not save a weak offer. (GHL “best copywriter”)

Build the deal on a Deal Sheet, no longer than a few lines. Sweeten it with things a real buyer would want: a free trial, easy payments, a money-back guarantee, a true free gift. (GHL “best copywriter”) I did not find him using the later phrase “risk reversal,” but his offer examples are that idea: let them try it, inspect it, or get their money back so the risk sits with you, not them.

Every good deal needs a believable reason why. If you say “have I got a deal for you” and give no reason, the deal is not believable. Common reasons he lists: going out of business, a fire, and — the one he likes most — “I Am Offering You This Deal Because You (By Virtue Of Some Unique Circumstance) Are So Special.” (BL Ch. 21) The reason must be true. Do not invent one.

The P.S.

Halbert almost always added a P.S. to the Boron Letters and to his newsletters. His own “Guru Gary’s Rolls Royce” letter in GHL “best copywriter” ends with a P.S. too. I did not find him stating a formal rule that “the P.S. is the second-most-read line.” Treat that common direct-mail claim as unconfirmed here. What the sources do show: he used the P.S. as a last personal beat — a reason to act, a next step, or a human note. Put a P.S. on letters and long emails. Use it to restate the deal or the next step, not to add fluff.

Prep, swipe habits, and testing

Prep before you write (GHL “best copywriter”):

  1. Confidential Fact Sheet. Saturate it. Put in the “bad stuff” too. He says amateurs skip this, and even many pros rush it. Aim for many pages of facts, not a few slogans.
  2. Benefit List. Walk the Fact Sheet and turn facts into benefits. Heavy car → safer, smoother. Light car → a reason for good mileage. Dozens of benefits, not three.
  3. Deal Sheet. The strongest true proposition, reduced to a few lines.

Then go to your swipe file — a box of good ads and letters, plus headline cards — and pull the best pieces in the same category. Do not steal them. Hand-copy the great ones “word-for-word in your own handwriting,” again and again, so good writing becomes automatic. (GHL “best copywriter”) Keep the swipe file current. Read the classic books. Watch what the leaders mail. (BL Ch. 23)

Read your copy out loud. Rewrite every place you stumble. Repeat until it is smooth. (BL Ch. 22)

Test small, then roll out (BL Ch. 22):

  1. Find the market.
  2. Get a product to sell to that market.
  3. Create the promotion.
  4. Test 1,000 to 5,000 pieces.
  5. Analyze the results.
  6. If it works, mail 20,000 to 100,000 more.
  7. If it still works, roll out and take care of business.

In GHL “improve response,” he also says: even if you will roll out in bulk, test in first class so you can read results fast (he says you will know within 12 days of the drop). Contact list owners. Ask for the hottest segments (multi-buyers, hotline names, big-ticket buyers).

Famous ads, and why he said they worked

The Coat-of-Arms letter

Halbert wrote a one-page letter selling a family-name research report: a short history of the last name plus a black-and-white drawing of the earliest known coat of arms for that name. He signed later versions in his wife’s name (Nancy L. Halbert). The letter opens like a personal note, not a pitch. One opening, reprinted in GHL “AIDA,” begins: “Did you know that your family name was recorded with a coat-of-arms in ancient heraldic archives more than seven centuries ago?”

Results he reports in BL Ch. 18: a simple one-page, 361-word letter that generated 7,156,000 cash-with-order customers. In GHL “grabbers” he calls it, in his view, the most widely mailed letter, and mentions a figure of 140 million pieces, all sent first class. That 140-million figure is his claim; I do not have postal receipts.

A later full-color catalog of crest products to those same buyers “bombed” and did not return mailing costs. A simple follow-up letter with a snapshot of the plaque, written in a personal voice (“Dear Mr. Noble, I thought you might like to see what the Noble coat-of-arms looks like in full color…”), is the piece he says brought in $40 million. (BL Ch. 18) Why it worked, in his words: it did not look like a sales job, and the offer was simple and personal.

The used-car letters he told people to hand-copy

In GHL “best copywriter,” he reprints three auto pitches and tells readers to copy them by hand:

  • Joe Karbo’s “Cadillac” letter (signed “Roger Atbury”). A short personal letter: leaving for the service, a new Cadillac, a cut price, take over the payments, “have your mechanic check it out,” drive it a couple of days, full tank, take it back if you don’t like it, no obligation, and an honest knock on the “awful green color.” Risk sits with the seller. The close is one phone number and a time window.
  • Ogilvy’s Rolls-Royce ad (see Part 3). Halbert reprints it as a model of fact-filled selling.
  • “Guru Gary’s Rolls Royce” letter. Halbert’s own letter selling pre-owned Rolls-Royces. He says that after mailing it one time, “the client couldn’t get enough cars to sell.” I do not have the client’s sales file; that result is Halbert’s report.

Re-headlining

His 475% lift from a new headline (GHL “killer headlines”) is the practical lesson: if an ad is weak, test a new headline before you junk the body. He also said some people pay him mainly to write headlines, and that he spends more creative effort there than on any other part of the work.

3. The Ogilvy brain

David Mackenzie Ogilvy (1911–1999) built Ogilvy & Mather. He wrote Confessions of an Advertising Man (1963) and Ogilvy on Advertising (1983). He also wrote a house ad, “How to Create Advertising That Sells,” that lists 38 research rules, and a 1982 staff memo called “How to Write.”

Fair warning on the books: I used published chapter notes (Ryan Delaney; Jeff Zych) plus circulated excerpts, not a page-by-page reading of the printed books. HCATS and the 1982 memo were read in full from public transcriptions. Where a number differs between sources, I show both.

“We sell, or else.”

When I write an advertisement, I don’t want you to tell me that you find it ‘creative.’ I want you to find it so interesting that you buy the product. — OoA Ch. 1, via Delaney’s notes

You cannot bore people into buying. (HCATS #6; Conf notes) He found no link between creative awards and sales, so his agency gave an award for the campaign that added the most sales. “Make the product the hero of your advertising.” (HCATS #8)

A line often quoted from his agency culture, in Jonathan Richardson’s Confessions notes: “We sell – or else.” I have that as a culture point in the notes, not as a page scan of the book.

Positioning, the promise, and the big idea

Positioning is the most important decision. Decide what the product is and who it is for before you write. Schweppes: soft drink or mixer? Dove: dry skin, or a cleaner for hands? “The result of your campaign depends less on how we write your advertising than on how your product is positioned.” (HCATS #1; spelling cleaned from a noisy scan, sense checked against other transcriptions)

A large, unique promise. “A promise is not a claim, or a theme, or a slogan. It is a benefit for the consumer.” Promise one benefit that is unique and competitive, and the product must deliver it. He quotes Samuel Johnson: “Promise, large promise, is the soul of an advertisement.” (HCATS #2) Boil the strategy down to one simple promise “and go the whole hog in delivering that promise.” Campaigns that try to please a committee achieve nothing. (HCATS #11)

The big idea. “Unless your advertising is built on a BIG IDEA it will pass like a ship in the night.” (HCATS #4) In OoA Ch. 2 (Delaney notes) he says no idea is big unless it will work for thirty years, and he gives five tests:

  1. Did it make me gasp when I first saw it?
  2. Do I wish I had thought of it myself?
  3. Is it unique?
  4. Does it fit the strategy to perfection?
  5. Could it be used for 30 years?

His Hathaway eyepatch ran for decades, which is the kind of run he had in mind. (HCATS #4 names “twenty years” for the eyepatch; OoA’s five-question test uses 30 years.)

Do your homework. Make the product the hero.

You do not stand “a tinker’s chance” of writing a good ad unless you do your homework. Study the product. Study what rivals have run, and with what success. Learn the customer’s words, what matters to them, and which promise would most likely make them buy. (OoA Ch. 2, Delaney notes)

There are no dull products, only dull writers. — OoA Ch. 2, via Delaney’s notes

If you and your rivals all make excellent products, do not imply yours is better. “Just say what’s good about your product — and do a clearer, more honest, more informative job of saying it.” (OoA Ch. 2, Delaney notes)

Headlines carry the ad

On the average, five times as many people read the headline as read the body copy. — HCATS #25; also OoA Ch. 7 and Conf Ch. 6

Both HCATS and Confessions (Ch. 6 excerpt) then say: if you do not sell in the headline, you have wasted 80 percent of your money (in Confessions: “you have spent eighty cents out of your dollar”). Jeff Zych’s notes on OoA Ch. 7 quote the later book as “wasted 90 per cent of your money.” The 5-times figure is stable. The “wasted money” figure is 80% in the earlier ad and book, and 90% in the 1983 book notes. I am not collapsing those into one number.

Rules that show up again and again (HCATS #25–31; OoA Ch. 7 notes; Conf Ch. 6 excerpt):

  • Put the brand and the promise in the headline.
  • Headlines that promise a benefit sell more than those that don’t.
  • Inject genuine news: a new product, a new improvement, or a new use. Don’t bury news in the body.
  • Telegraph the message in simple language. No puns, double meanings, or obscure lines. Readers will not stop to decode them.
  • Length: In a department-store test, headlines of 10 words or longer sold more than short ones. For recall, 8–10 words did best. In mail order, 6–12 words got the most coupon returns. “On the average, long headlines sell more merchandise than short ones.” His Rolls-Royce line is the example. (HCATS #29) OoA Ch. 7 notes also report a Starch finding that headlines of more than 10 words get less readership than short ones, and then conclude: if you need a long headline, write one; if you want a short one, that’s fine too. Use the length the promise needs.
  • Localize: in local ads, put the city in the headline. (HCATS #30)
  • Flag the prospect: “MOTHERS, BED-WETTERS, GOING TO EUROPE?” (HCATS #31)
  • Do not use a blind headline that hides the product and the benefit. In OoA Ch. 7 (chapter text excerpt) he says they are much weaker — the circulated excerpt cuts off mid-sentence, so I will not finish a number I cannot see.
  • Do not use cheap negative tricks in the headline. This is a standard Ogilvy guide in the chapter notes; I am restating it, not offering a fresh verbatim line I did not have on the page.
  • A headline in quotation marks lifts recall. OoA Ch. 7 chapter text (circulated) says “an average of 28 per cent.” If you see 26% elsewhere, that is a different citation; I am sticking to 28% from that chapter text.

In Confessions Ch. 6 he says he never writes fewer than sixteen headlines for a single ad, and that a change of headline can make a difference of ten to one in sales. (Conf Ch. 6 excerpt) The two most powerful words, he says, are FREE and NEW. You can seldom use FREE. You can almost always use NEW if you try. Other “shopworn” words that still work in measured mail-order ads include HOW TO, SUDDENLY, NOW, ANNOUNCING, INTRODUCING, AMAZING, OFFER, QUICK, EASY, ADVICE TO, THE TRUTH ABOUT, COMPARE, LAST CHANCE. “Don’t turn up your nose at these clichés. They may be shopworn, but they work.” (Conf Ch. 6 excerpt)

Body copy, long copy, and specifics

Write as one person talking to one person, “second person singular.” (OoA Ch. 7, Zych notes) Short sentences and short paragraphs. Plain words. Get to the point. Do not write a philosophy essay. “Tell your reader what your product will do for him or her, and tell it with specifics.” Avoid analogies; people misread them. Avoid superlatives and “brag and boast.” Make the first paragraph a grabber. Use subheads so people can scan. (OoA Ch. 7 notes; Conf Ch. 6)

A line often attributed to him, “Tell the truth, but make the truth fascinating,” is widely repeated. I did not pin the exact sentence to a page in the notes I had, so treat that wording as a paraphrase of his stance, not a confirmed quote.

Yes, people read long copy. Readership falls off rapidly up to fifty words, but drops very little between fifty and five hundred words. (This page contains 1909 words, and you are reading it.) — HCATS #32

“The more you tell, the more you sell.” (HCATS #32) In OoA (Delaney notes) he says that for many products, long copy sells more than short, that direct-response advertisers know this from split-run tests, and that if you want long copy to be read, “your first paragraph should be a grabber.” He also says, with no research behind this part, that long copy can signal that you have something important to say, even to people who do not read it all.

Be specific. “The consumer isn’t a moron; she is your wife.” (HCATS #13, in the problem-solution rule; also a culture line in Conf notes.) Name percentages, times, measured results. His famous Rolls-Royce ad is the model: one fact in the headline, then a long list of numbered facts in the body.

At 60 miles an hour the loudest noise in this new Rolls-Royce comes from the electric clock. — Headline, Rolls-Royce ad, reprinted in GHL “best copywriter” and in HCATS #29. Word order varies slightly by reprint (“At 60” / “At Sixty”); the fact is the same.

The body opens with an engineer saying there is “no magic about it — it is merely patient attention to detail,” then stacks numbered facts (mufflers, body finish, and so on). Halbert notes that numbered-fact counts vary by reprint, so I will not lock in a single count. The method is the point: one true, surprising fact in the headline; proof in a list of checkable facts; no empty adjectives.

Helpful information gets more readers. OoA Ch. 7 notes say headlines that offer helpful information, like “HOW TO WIN FRIENDS AND INFLUENCE PEOPLE,” attract above-average readership. A “75% more readers” figure is often attached to this idea in secondary notes. I could not confirm that exact percent in HCATS or in the chapter text I had, so I am not using it.

Use real testimonials from customers or relevant experts. Avoid irrelevant celebrities; they steal attention and look bought. (HCATS #12; OoA Ch. 7 notes)

On price: Ogilvy says to include it. Zych’s OoA notes quote the jewelry-window analogy: if the price is not shown, people turn the page. (OoA Ch. 7 notes) If a client forbids a price, use an honest stand-in (a payment line, a range, or “ask for the quote”) — but know you are breaking his default rule on purpose.

Brand image and good manners

Every ad should add to “the complex symbol which is the brand image.” Most ads are made ad hoc, so the brand has no steady personality. The maker who builds the sharpest personality, he says, gets the largest share. (HCATS #3) Image is personality: name, pack, price, ad style, and the product itself. (OoA Ch. 2 notes)

If the ad looks ugly, people will think the product is shoddy. (HCATS #5) A culture line in Richardson’s Confessions notes: “Never run an ad you wouldn’t want your family to see.” Don’t clown. First-class business, in a first-class way.

Pictures, captions, and print layout

  • Photographs beat drawings, “almost invariably”: more readers, more belief, better memory, more coupons, more sales. (HCATS #35)
  • Story appeal: a photo that makes the reader ask “What goes on here?” leads them into the copy. Harold Rudolph called this “story appeal.” (HCATS #33)
  • Before-and-after, and any “visualized contrast,” is above average. (HCATS #34)
  • Twice as many people read captions as body copy, so every photo gets a caption that is a mini ad: brand plus promise. (HCATS #36)
  • Editorial layouts beat “addy” layouts. (HCATS #37)
  • Readers look first at the picture, then the headline, then the copy — put them in that order. (OoA Ch. 7, Zych notes)

Television and video

From HCATS #13–24 and OoA Ch. 8 notes:

  • Open with an exciting first moment (“grabbers”). Quiet openings lose the audience. (HCATS #24)
  • Problem-solution works if you don’t cheat: show a problem the viewer knows, solve it, and prove it. (HCATS #13)
  • Honest visual demos work. (HCATS #14)
  • “Slice of life” playlets are corny, and writers hate them, but they still sell. (HCATS #15)
  • Avoid “logorrhea” (a torrent of words). What you show matters more than what you say. (HCATS #16)
  • On-camera voice beats voice-over. (HCATS #17)
  • Musical backgrounds, on average, reduced recall in their tests. (HCATS #18)
  • Animation is less persuasive for adults; it can help with children. (HCATS #21)
  • Factual commercials tended to beat emotional ones, though he also ran successful emotional work (he names Maxwell House and Hershey). (HCATS #23)
  • Name the brand early and often, and state the benefit early. (OoA Ch. 8 covers TV in more depth in the notes; use this with the points above.)

Testing, and repeating winners

“Scores of great advertisements have been discarded before they have begun to pay off. Readership can actually increase with repetition — up to five repetitions.” (HCATS #38) Measure selling effectiveness at regular intervals, and keep running a campaign until research shows it has worn out. (OoA Ch. 2 notes) Split-run headlines. Test promises, not just decorations.

Do not name competitors, in his print advice: it confuses readers and can make the rival the hero. (OoA Ch. 7, Zych notes)

The 1982 “How to Write” memo

On September 7, 1982, Ogilvy sent an internal memo to everyone at Ogilvy & Mather, titled “How to Write.” It was later printed in The Unpublished David Ogilvy (1986). Public transcriptions (The Marginalian; Farnam Street; Open Culture) agree on this text:

The better you write, the higher you go in Ogilvy & Mather. People who think well, write well. Woolly minded people write woolly memos, woolly letters and woolly speeches. Good writing is not a natural gift. You have to learn to write well. Here are 10 hints:
  1. Read the Roman-Raphaelson book on writing. Read it three times. (That’s Writing That Works, by Kenneth Roman and Joel Raphaelson.)
  2. Write the way you talk. Naturally.
  3. Use short words, short sentences and short paragraphs.
  4. Never use jargon words like reconceptualize, demassification, attitudinally, judgmentally. They are hallmarks of a pretentious ass.
  5. Never write more than two pages on any subject.
  6. Check your quotations.
  7. Never send a letter or a memo on the day you write it. Read it aloud the next morning — and then edit it.
  8. If it is something important, get a colleague to improve it.
  9. Before you send your letter or your memo, make sure it is crystal clear what you want the recipient to do.
  10. If you want ACTION, don’t write. Go and tell the guy what you want.

Hint 5 is a memo rule, not an ad rule. His ads often used long copy (HCATS #32). Use hint 5 for emails and memos. Use long copy in ads when the facts earn it.

A note on “38 headline types”

Ogilvy did not publish a list called “38 types of headlines.” What he did publish is the 38 research rules in “How to Create Advertising That Sells.” Only some of those rules are about headlines (mainly #25–31). His headline guides live in Confessions Ch. 6 and OoA Ch. 7: benefit, news, how-to / helpful information, localized, flagged prospect, quoted, long vs. short, and a list of working words. If someone hands you a sheet titled “Ogilvy’s 38 headline types,” it is almost certainly a mix-up with the 38-point ad. Do not treat that sheet as his.

The 38 HCATS rules, in short

Strategy (#1–11): position first; large unique promise; brand image; big idea; first-class look; don’t be a bore; innovate (and pretest); be suspicious of awards; psychological segments; don’t bury news; one promise, go the whole hog.

TV (#12–24): credible testimonials, no irrelevant celebrities; problem-solution (don’t cheat); honest demos; slice of life; no logorrhea; on-camera voice; go easy on music beds; honest stand-ups; a “burr of singularity”; animation is weak for adults; salvage weak spots in the edit; factual usually beats emotional; exciting openings.

Print (#25–38): sell in the headline (brand + promise); benefit; news; simple words; long headlines can sell; localize; flag the prospect; people do read long copy; story-appeal photos; before-and-after; photos over art; captions that sell; editorial layouts; repeat winners.

4. Where they agree, and where they differ

They agree

  • Sales beat cleverness. Halbert: write for money, not applause. Ogilvy: “we sell, or else”; awards do not equal sales.
  • The first glance does most of the work. Halbert: the sale is largely made when they see the ad and the headline. Ogilvy: five times as many people read the headline as the body.
  • Specifics beat adjectives. Halbert wants “77.6% of all car owners,” not “most car owners.” (BL Ch. 23) Ogilvy wants numbered facts, not “brag and boast.”
  • Flag the right reader. Halbert customizes (“L.A. Real Estate,” “L.A. chiropractors”). Ogilvy flags the group and names the city.
  • Plain talk. Both want short words, short sentences, and a voice that sounds like a person. Both say to read copy out loud (BL Ch. 22; Memo 1982, hint 7).
  • Homework first. Halbert’s Fact / Benefit / Deal sheets. Ogilvy’s product study, rival study, and customer language.
  • Test, then keep the winner. Halbert tests 1,000–5,000 and rolls out. Ogilvy split-runs headlines and repeats ads until they wear out.
  • Honesty. Halbert hates bait headlines. Ogilvy says don’t cheat in a demo, and don’t run an ad you wouldn’t show your family.

5. The combined playbook: write any ad, letter, page, or script

Use this sequence every time. Do not skip the prep. Brand and legal rules still come first.

Step 1. Pick the starving crowd

Who has already shown they want this result? What did they buy last? What words do they use? Where do they live? If you cannot name a hungry group in one sentence, you are not ready to write. (BL Ch. 6; GHL “The One Advantage”)

Step 2. Lock the position and the one promise

What is the product, and who is it for? What one benefit will you go all in on? If a committee wants five messages, you have no promise. Check it with Ogilvy’s big-idea tests if the campaign must last. (HCATS #1, #2, #4, #11)

Step 3. Build the three sheets

Handwrite (or type, then print) a Confidential Fact Sheet, a Benefit List, and a Deal Sheet. Put in the weak points. Translate every useful fact into a benefit in the reader’s life. Sweeten the deal with a true trial, guarantee, or easy way to pay. Write the reason why the deal exists — a real one. (GHL “best copywriter”; BL Ch. 21)

Step 4. Steal structure, not sentences

Open the swipe file. Pull winning ads in the same category. Hand-copy one or two so the rhythm gets into your hands. Do not lift claims, names, or proof that are not yours. (GHL “best copywriter”; BL Ch. 23)

Step 5. Write 16 or more headlines (or hooks)

Flag the reader. Put in the brand and the promise, or news plus a benefit. Be specific. Localize if it is a local offer. No puns, no bait, no blind lines. Pick the one that would sell even if the body never got read. (Conf Ch. 6; HCATS #25–31; GHL “killer headlines”)

Step 6. Write the body in AIDA order

Attention: relevant hook or picture. Interest: a true story and at least two interesting facts. Desire: a word picture of the result in their life, plus proof (a real testimonial with consent, an honest demo, a certification, or an exact sourced figure). Action: one clear next step, repeated, with what they gain by acting and lose by waiting. For a letter, add a P.S. that restates the deal or the step. (BL Ch. 15–16; HCATS #12–14)

Step 7. Fit the format

Print or static: photo (not cheap art), caption that is a mini ad, editorial layout, brand + promise in the headline. (HCATS #33–37)

Letter or email: A-pile look. Personal salutation. Short paragraphs. A clear close and a P.S. (BL Ch. 10–11, 16)

Landing page: same as a long ad. Headline sells alone. First screen = hook, promise, and proof. The button tells the exact next step. Repeat the close.

Video or script: grab in the first moment. Brand early. Promise early. Problem, solution, honest demo. On-camera voice if you can. Captions on. No cheating. Say and show the close. (HCATS #13–17, #24)

Step 8. Edit like both men

Read it out loud. Cut every stumble. Cut jargon. Swap “procure” for “get.” Check every quote and number. Sleep on it if you can, then edit again. Ask: would I show this to my family? Is any grabber a cheap shot? (BL Ch. 17, 22; Memo 1982; HCATS #5, #13; BL Ch. 14)

Step 9. Score it, then test it

Run the 14-point checklist in Part 7. Score Halbert and Ogilvy separately. Fix anything under an 8 before you spend. Test a small slice. Change one big thing at a time (often the headline or the offer). Kill losers. Repeat winners until they wear out. (BL Ch. 22; HCATS #38)

1–10 scoring rubric

Score Halbert and Ogilvy separately, on their own principles. Start at 10. Drop to the first band that fits.

ScoreWhat it means
10Every checklist item for that lens passes. One clear promise, proof you can check, nothing to cut, nothing invented. You would hand-copy it into a swipe file.
9One small miss (brand not in the headline, or a slightly soft close).
8Strong promise and proof, plus 1–2 craft misses (headline short on the benefit, body a bit crowded).

8. A short worked example (a made-up product)

This example is fiction, used only to show the method. The car, the owner, the miles, the price, and the “reason why” are made up. In real work you would replace every fact with something you can prove, and you would not publish a testimonial you did not get in writing.

The job: a private owner in Austin, Texas, is selling a 2016 Honda Civic. She bought a truck for work and does not need two cars. She wants a clean letter she can print and a short post she can run in a local group.

Prep (the three sheets, shortened)

Fact sheet (sample facts)
  • 2016 Honda Civic LX, 4-door, silver
  • 68,400 miles (shown on the odometer)
  • One owner, no accidents (she would only say this if the title and history report agree)
  • New tires in March; oil changes every 5,000 miles at the same shop
  • Small parking scrape on the rear bumper, not repaired
  • Clean inside; non-smoker
  • Asking $11,400
  • True reason: she bought a used Tacoma last week and needs the driveway
Benefits + deal
  • Light, easy to park, cheap to run (she would add real MPG only if she has it)
  • One-owner history is easy to check
  • New tires = a cost the next owner does not have to eat right away
  • The scrape is disclosed, so the ad feels honest
  • Deal: $11,400. Bring a mechanic or a shop; she will meet you there. Drive it for a day. If you don’t want it, bring it back with the same miles, no hard feelings. First person with cash or a cashier’s check gets it, because she really does need the driveway.

Headlines she would test (she would write 16; here are 5)

  1. Austin: one-owner 2016 Civic, 68,400 miles, $11,400. Bring your mechanic.
  2. I bought a truck last week. My 2016 Civic needs a new driveway in Austin.
  3. 2016 Honda Civic, 68,400 miles, new tires in March. Take it to your shop before you decide.
  4. Silver 2016 Civic in Austin — I’ll tell you about the bumper scrape before you drive over.
  5. How to buy a one-owner Civic in Austin without guessing about the miles or the scrape.

Headline 1 is the safest Ogilvy line: city, brand, exact facts, price, and the risk reversal. Headline 2 is the Halbert “reason why” plus a human open. A fair test is 1 against 2.

The letter (AIDA, A-pile voice)

Dear Neighbor,

I bought a used Tacoma last week for work, and now I have two cars and one driveway. The car I need to sell is my 2016 Honda Civic LX. It is silver, four doors, and it has 68,400 miles on the odometer as of Sunday.

I am the only owner. It has no accident record on the history report I pulled this week, and I will hand you that report when you come. I have the oil done every 5,000 miles at the same shop on Lamar, and I put new tires on it in March. The inside is clean. I don’t smoke.

I also want you to know about the one ding: there is a small parking scrape on the rear bumper. I did not paint over it. You will see it in the first minute.

The price is $11,400. If you want it, bring cash or a cashier’s check. Before you decide, I will meet you at your mechanic, or at a shop you pick, and I will let you drive it for a day. If it is not what you wanted, bring it back with the same miles. No argument.

If you’d like to see it, text me at [her number] and tell me a time this week after 5 p.m. I can meet you in North Austin with a full tank.

Thanks,
Maya R.

P.S. The first person who is happy with the inspection and has the check can take it the same day. I really do need the driveway back.

Why this scores well

Halbert
  • A real reason why (the truck and the driveway), not “act now!!”
  • Looks like a person, not a dealer ad
  • Exact details (68,400; March tires; the scrape)
  • Desire is practical: a car you can check, not a fantasy
  • Deal + risk reversal + a hand-led close + a P.S.
Ogilvy
  • Brand, facts, and price are in the lead headline
  • City flagged (“Austin”)
  • No adjectives standing in for proof
  • The scrape is the opposite of brag-and-boast, which makes the rest easier to believe
  • A complete sell in one page; first-class manners

A weak version of the same ad would say “GREAT DEAL on a CLEAN Civic!!! Won’t last!!!” with no miles, no price, no scrape, and no way to inspect it. That fails items 3, 7, 8, 9, 10, and 14, and it would be capped at 5 for empty urgency.

If Maya were shooting a 30-second video: open on the driveway with two cars; say “I bought this truck last week, so my Civic needs a home”; show the odometer, the new tires, and the bumper scrape in honest light; say the price and “bring your mechanic”; end with the text number on screen and out loud.
