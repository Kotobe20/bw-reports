Playbook: Derick Carneiro's "4 Sales" Cold-Traffic Ad Method

0. What this is

POST An X Article titled "how I write ads that make strangers buy" by Derick Carneiro (@DerickCarneiro_), posted Nov 19, 2026. Bio: "$40M in 6mo @ 22. Wrote the #1 supp VSLs ($300M+)". In the post he says he is 22, has written ads and VSLs that sold over $300M (his claim, we cannot verify), and trained "90% of the copywriters and creative strategists in Brazil". It is his first article on his ad creation process. Engagement when we read it: about 205K impressions, 2.1K likes, 6.3K bookmarks, 120 replies.

POST The article's thesis, in his own summary: a cold-traffic ad does not sell the product. It makes 4 sales before the product, and every sale is made with reasons the avatar already wants to hear. The process for finding those reasons is always the same: Premises -> Target -> Brief -> Writing. Then: minimize risk where leverage is low, raise the bet where leverage is high.

POST The example ad is for a supplement brand (Aurivita Cayenne Pepper, aimed at men with diabetes and erectile dysfunction). The example is health-supplement specific. The process is what transfers, not the claims.

1. Core ideas

1.1 Who this is for POST

Cold traffic: a market of strangers who know they have a problem, may know a solution or two, but have not solved it, and get more frustrated and skeptical with every thing that fails. Opening with price, product or offer is hard to scale with them. Use indirect ads: grab attention -> hold interest -> explain a new mechanism -> only then product, price, offer.

1.2 The 4 sales every ad must make POST

None of them is "the product."

  1. Content Sale: get attention and make them want to hear the ad to the end.
  2. Hope Sale: show what has been stopping them, the action that must happen to fix it, and that a specific (rare/unique) mechanism performs that action.
  3. Value Sale: present the product as the best/only place to get that mechanism, in a way that raises the odds of success, makes the "dream scenario" tangible, shortens time to results, minimizes effort, at a price that feels far lower than the cost of never solving it.
  4. Impulse Sale: the risk of NOT taking the offer is bigger than the risk of taking it, and they gain a lot buying now and lose a lot waiting.

A sale = persuasion = presenting an idea plus reasons why it is true. Those reasons are the "Ad Psychology."

1.3 The 17 elements of Ad Psychology POST

Stage 1: Content Sale

  1. Format: why the avatar relaxes and does not suspect a sales pitch.
  2. Topic: why they might care about what the ad says.
  3. Concept: why they feel compelled to watch to the end.
  4. Visual Hook: why they snap out of autopilot and stop.
  5. Copy Hook: why they decide to keep watching.

Stage 2: Hope Sale

  1. Identification: why they feel understood.
  2. Point of Contact: what they already suspect is causing the problem.
  3. Unique Mechanism of the Problem (UMP): the missing reason that proves the suspicion right and is the real cause.
  4. Unique Mechanism of the Function: the action needed to fix it, and why other solutions cannot do it.
  5. Unique Mechanism of the Solution (UMS): the one solution that performs that action, with proof.
  6. Conditions: why other solutions do not offer it; what makes it rare/hard to get.

Stage 3: Value Sale

  1. Unique Selling Proposition: why this product is the best/only place to get the mechanism under the right conditions.
  2. Features: components and attributes, each tied to a lever (odds, dream scenario, effort, time).
  3. Anchoring: why not solving it costs more (money, emotion, time) than the price.

Stage 4: Impulse Sale

  1. Risk Reversal: why NOT buying is riskier than buying.
  2. What do they gain (by buying now)?
  3. What do they lose (by buying later)?

1.4 The skill that actually matters POST

Knowing the 17 elements is not enough. The skill is choosing the right reasons (highest odds of working). The "big insight": your job is not to say what you think needs to be said. Find WHAT the avatar wants to hear, HOW they want to hear it, and WHO they want to hear it from, and put it all in one place.

1.5 Risk philosophy POST

Like a poker player: minimize risk in low-leverage stages (market, segment, research, psychographics) by reusing what is already working; raise the bet in high-leverage stages (concept, format, hook), because a new concept/format/hook in a hot market is worth more than finding a brand-new market. Risk of an ad is proportional to the number of bets in it. For scaling brands he runs a few conservative-risk ads, many moderate-risk ads, and some aggressive-risk ads, so fresh ads keep flowing; one winning bet pays for the losers.

1.6 Research stance POST

Do NOT spend hours/days on deep psychographic research: it mostly yields the same stuff and rarely changes the winning ad. Instead steal other people's research: the winners (yours and competitors') in the same niche already encode the right pains, fears, desires, beliefs and customer language. Psychographics change little over time (his example: the man with ED in 2016 has the same fears as in 2026), so old winning VSLs, advertorials and ads from any year are valid research. Put the most research energy into identity, especially behavior (the news, videos, topics and trends that genuinely grab the avatar's attention), because that is where the best topics, formats, concepts and hooks come from.

2. The process, step by step

Step 1: Premises ("what do we know?") POST

Collect facts about the brand and product, usually from the product page:

  1. Brand name 2. Product name 3. Ingredients 4. Attributes (testing, facility, servings, usage, results timeline, shipping, side-effect notes) 5. Price/bundles 6. Guarantee 7. What they gain by buying now 8. What they lose by waiting.

Step 2: Target ("who are we talking to?") POST

Answer 3 questions.

Q1. Which market? A market = a group sharing one desire (e.g., weight loss). List the markets the product could serve, then pick ONE.

  • Brand already running: find their highest-traffic landers (the market the top lander targets is their best position); download and analyze all their ads from the last 60 days (he says this is easy with the GetHookd MCP + Claude) and have Claude report which market appears most.
  • New brand/product: in GetHookd find at least 3 competitors per candidate market (fewer than 3 = cut the market); check traffic volume and growth of those brands on TrendTrack; pick the market with the most combined 30-day traffic growth (a tie or tiny difference: pick either).

Q2. Which segment? A segment = a cluster inside the market with a shared condition, identity or behavior (e.g., market weight loss -> postpartum women). Two ways: (1) copy segments already working for you/competitors; (2) manual Reddit research for under-served segments. In his example: market = ED, segment = men with diabetes and ED (because many of the brand's ED ads targeted it).

Q3. Who is the avatar? Two layers:

  • Psychographics: primary desire, problem, secondary desires, pain points, dreams, fears.
  • Identity: demographics (sex, age, nationality, language, race/ethnicity, religion, marital status, family role); behavior (which competitor ads catch them, which general videos, trending topics, hobbies, news, movies/TV, other health conditions); perspective (beliefs, biases, enemies, allies, language).

Step 3: Brief ("what do we say, and how?") POST

Fill in all 17 elements with what the avatar wants to hear. His sequence:

A. Mechanism (Hope Sale)

  1. Point of contact: what does this avatar already blame? (Example: "too much sugar reduces blood flow.")
  2. Problem mechanism: the cause they are most ready to accept, connecting dots they already believe (so you do not have to convince them of anything new).
  3. Function mechanism: the action that must happen, and why other solutions cannot do it. He found this via behavior research (a news story plus a high-volume search about mixing the common pill with metformin), then positioned "the common pill was not made for men with diabetes."
  4. Solution mechanism: introduce the ingredient/solution, what it does, how it fixes the root cause.

B. Content (Content Sale)

  1. Topic: facts/subjects the avatar cares about (he leans on search demand and news; he usually lists many topics).
  2. Format: (a) what should the ad look like; (b) narrative style that holds attention; (c) characters and roles. He reused the setting/dynamic of one proven ad and the presenter style of another, then made a high-leverage bet on a new visual style tied to a cultural moment (GTA VI aesthetic).
  3. Concept: characters + narrative + topic = a one-line "logline", then an angle that creates "an uncontrollable urge to hear more" (his: a fear-based "don't mix X with Y unless you want to lose Z").
  4. Copy hook: pick a proven hook structure first (he used "This is what happens to ___ when you ___"), then fill it with the avatar's own slang.
  5. Visual hook: something congruent with the format that still breaks expectations and stops the scroll (his: a shocking prop held up to match the copy hook).

C. Offer (Value Sale)

  1. Conditions: why can't they just get this at the store / do it themselves? ("The problem is not finding the ingredient; it is getting it in the body the right way.") Do not skip this or they will think "I'll just buy it at the grocery store."
  2. USP = simply the answer to the Conditions. Show the product delivers, one by one, the conditions you just said are necessary. Do not invent a differentiator.
  3. Features: do not list ingredient by ingredient ("nobody buys hawthorn"). Every feature must answer one of four levers: Odds (why it works this time), Dream scenario (make it tangible), Effort (minimize), Time (a timeline that makes sense). If it answers none, it stays out of the ad. Do not promise instant results; explain why it takes time.
  4. Anchoring: break the price down per day, then compare to the cost of living with the problem: financial (money wasted on things that do not work), emotional, and time (it keeps getting worse).

D. Close (Impulse Sale) The close is not squeezing; it is showing that buying now is the safest decision.

  1. Risk reversal: not "we have a guarantee" (everyone does) but "the guarantee window is longer than the time you need to prove it on yourself." (His example: 120-day guarantee vs 10-week recommended use.)
  2. Gain: spell out bundle by bundle what extra they get now (free units, free/same-day shipping).
  3. Lose: tie to real timing (his: Black Friday half-price, stock running out, no ETA, price goes back up).

Step 4: Writing POST

By now writing is "filling in the blanks."

  • Way 1 (by hand): download and transcribe a proven reference ad; split it into mini-blocks by the job each does (hook, belief break, mechanism reveal, product presentation, etc.); write your brief points into those blocks.
  • Way 2 (AI): same, but have a model extract the mini-blocks, give it the brief + reference, and use a strong writing model (he names Claude, "StefanBrain" and DeepSeek).
  • Key rule: the brief decides WHAT is said; the reference decides only the ORDER and RHYTHM. Most people who "let AI write it" fail because they skip the brief and expect the model to guess the psychology.

3. Template / script structure from the post (the worked example)

POST The full final ad copy was shared in a Google Doc (public, we read it). Its skeleton, with the example's topic replaced by <...> placeholders so you can see the structure (this is OUR abstraction of his script's order; his actual wording is in his doc):

  1. Copy hook (structure: "This is what happens to when you .")
  2. Bold claim + promise to explain (" should be for ... I'm going to explain why right now. Watch...")
  3. Point of contact / UMP (what the body/process needs; what is breaking it)
  4. Function mechanism (what the common solution does and does not do; why it fails for this segment: "That's why it works for so many people, and not for you.")
  5. Dismiss other attempts (what they already tried that only forces a short-term reaction)
  6. UMS (the ingredient/solution and how it fixes the root cause)
  7. Conditions (why you can't just get it yourself; the 3 things it needs to work)
  8. CTA 1 + USP ("If this video helped, tap the link below..." then the product delivering each condition one by one)
  9. Features tied to levers (odds / dream / effort: "no new diet, and you keep your X")
  10. Guarantee as risk reversal
  11. Bundle gain + anchoring (price per day vs. the cost of the thing that does not work)
  12. Scarcity / lose-by-waiting + final CTA

One-line prompts you can reuse OURS: adapted from the post's questions

Brief-builder prompt for an AI:

You are building an ad brief, not writing the ad yet.
Premises: <paste product facts, price, guarantee, gains, losses>.
Market: <market>. Segment: <segment>. Avatar: <1 paragraph>.
Using ONLY what this avatar already believes and wants to hear, fill in the 17 elements:
Format, Topic, Concept, Visual Hook, Copy Hook, Identification, Point of Contact,
Unique Mechanism of the Problem, of the Function, of the Solution, Conditions,
USP, Features (tag each as Odds/Dream/Effort/Time; drop any that fit none),
Anchoring, Risk Reversal, Gain, Lose.
Flag any claim that is not supported by the premises.

Writing prompt: 

Here is a reference ad split into mini-blocks (order and rhythm only). Here is the brief (what to say).
Write the script by filling each mini-block with the brief's content. Do not add claims that are not in the brief.

4. Quick decision rules (distilled) POST unless marked

  • Do not open cold ads with price/product/offer.
  • One market -> one segment -> one avatar per ad.
  • Pick the market where the brand/competitors already have traffic; do not gamble on new markets.
  • Spend research time on avatar behavior (what grabs their attention); steal the rest from winning ads.
  • Put your creative bets on concept, format, hook. Keep the market/segment/mechanism borrowed from winners.
  • Every feature must serve odds, dream, effort or time.
  • Guarantee framed as "enough time to prove it", not just "we have a guarantee."
  • Scarcity only if it is real.
  • Batch mix for scaling brands: a few conservative, many moderate, some aggressive.
  • Brief first, AI writing second.

5. How Biscayne Water applies this ALL OURS: suggestions, not from the post

Context: Biscayne Water sells and installs water filtration in Miami-Dade and Broward. All leads go to https://www.biscaynewater.com/quiz. Stack: Close CRM, Housecall Pro, Cortana, Slack. Offer: $99/mo with 0% financing. Viktor (Slack AI bot) owns the fact sheets, decks and scripts.

5.1 Important differences to respect

  1. Different product type. Derick sells a low-price supplement direct to cart. We sell a home install with a quiz -> appointment -> in-home close. The ad's job for us is to win the quiz completion (a lead), not a cart purchase. So "Impulse Sale" becomes "take the quiz now / book the visit," and "Risk Reversal" becomes whatever our real guarantee/warranty/free-test terms are.
  2. Claims discipline. His example makes health-cause claims. We should not copy that style. For water, only claim what our fact sheet and water-test data support. Viktor's fact sheet is the source of truth for premises and claims; nothing in this playbook is a verified claim about our product.
  3. Never use the post's example claims, scarcity or Black Friday pricing for us. Scarcity must be real (e.g., real install-slot capacity), never invented.

5.2 Mapping the 4 sales to our funnel

Sale In Derick's ad For Biscayne Water (OURS)
Content Visual hook + copy hook + topic Local, curiosity-first hook (e.g., about what is actually in Miami tap water or why a store filter is not enough). Use real numbers only from our water-test data.
Hope Mechanism: why other solutions failed, what action fixes it Why pitchers/fridge filters/store filters do not do the full job and what a whole-home or point-of-use system does differently (mechanism comes from Viktor's fact sheet)
Value USP, features by lever, anchoring Conditions: why you cannot just fix this yourself; features tied to odds/effort/time (professional install, no big upfront cost); anchor $99/mo vs. what they spend now on bottled water, filters, replacements
Impulse Guarantee, gain, lose 0% financing at $99/mo as gain; real guarantee/warranty as risk reversal; "free water test / quiz" as the low-risk step; real scarcity (install capacity, promo end dates) only if true

5.3 Step 1 for us: build a Premises sheet (owner: Viktor)

Create one page in the existing fact-sheet format with: brand, systems/products, what each does, certifications/test claims we can prove, install facts and timeline, price/$99/mo terms and 0% financing terms, warranty/guarantee, service area, real gains (promos), real losses (deadlines). Every later ad brief pastes from this page. Anything not on it cannot be said in an ad.

5.4 Step 2 for us: Target

  • Markets we could go after (OURS, to validate): drinking-water quality/taste, chlorine and chemical concerns, hard water/scale on fixtures and appliances, well water (we already have a well-water setter script), family health worries. Pick ONE using his rule: look at which topic our best-performing leads and Meta ads already come from (Close source data + Meta results + David's audit), and what competitors in South Florida run (Meta Ad Library, or GetHookd/TrendTrack if we subscribe).
  • Segments (OURS): new homeowners, parents of young kids, well-water homeowners, condo/HOA owners, Spanish-speaking households (our own data already shows Spanish Meta leads close well: 12.5% close rate, per the Nov 12 report). Choose segments from what is already working before inventing new ones.
  • Avatar behavior research (his highest-leverage research area): local news about water advisories/boil notices, local Facebook groups, Reddit threads (r/Miami, r/fort_lauderdale, water filter subs), search questions people type. Use these for topics.
  • Steal research from winners: our own best ads and the highest-engagement competitor ads; call recordings in Close/Cortana for customer language ("VOC").

5.5 Step 3 for us: Brief template for a Biscayne ad

Use the 17-element template (section 3 prompt). Fill the Mechanism from the fact sheet, the Offer from $99/mo + 0% financing, the Close from real guarantee and real scarcity. Keep the CTA going to https://www.biscaynewater.com/quiz.

5.6 Step 4 for us: Writing

  1. Pick 2 to 3 proven reference ads (our best UGC/VSL or a high-performing competitor ad in water/home services).
  2. Split into mini-blocks.
  3. Viktor or an AI drafts scripts by filling blocks from the brief (brief first, AI second).
  4. Run a claims check against the fact sheet before any video is produced or posted.

5.7 Batch strategy (his risk philosophy applied)

Each creative batch for Meta: conservative (same market/segment/concept/format as a proven winner, new hook), moderate (proven market + segment, new format or concept), aggressive (a new format or cultural/local-moment bet, still in a proven market). Test mix example: 2 conservative, 5 moderate, 2 aggressive per batch. Label every ad with its bets so we learn which bet won. (Ratio is OURS; Derick only says "a few / a lot / some.")

5.8 Where each tool plays

  • Close CRM: pull VOC (objections, phrases) from calls/notes; tag lead source by ad concept so we can see which concept/segment closes, not just which gets cheap leads (ties to the Close -> Cortana -> Meta tracking fix).
  • Cortana / Slack: share winning briefs and hooks in #admin with Viktor; Viktor keeps the Premises sheet and approves claims.
  • Housecall Pro: job-complete data (customer type, home type, water source) shows which segments are most profitable to target.
  • Quiz page: make the quiz question set mirror the mechanism (e.g., ask about water source and the problems they notice) so the ad promise and quiz feel like one story (continuity from the ad's point of contact).
