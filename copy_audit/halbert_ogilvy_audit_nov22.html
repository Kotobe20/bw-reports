Top 5 fixes

1. Pull or re-cut all 4 of Doug's videos before they spend another dollar.
All four break house rules: price anchors ("eight grand", "$99/mo"), "30% off" + "free installation" + "this month only", "lifetime warranty", fluoride and "licensed plumber" claims the site doesn't make, and a staged sludge demo. Rule violations cap every video at 5/10. The rewrites keep each video's format.
2. One promise per ad. Move the boilerplate to a single offer line.
Almost every static repeats the same stack in the body (financing + 1000+ + warranty + NSF + areas). Ogilvy calls that the minutes of a committee (HCATS #11), and it breaks Halbert's slippery slide. Body = one benefit + its proof. The financing line and "1000+ customers, 5 stars" go once, in the CTA/caption.
3. Make every headline sell on its own: brand + benefit (or news) + who/where.
5 times as many people read the headline as the body (HCATS #25). Blind or clever lines ("We're sorry…", "Florida sun. Florida water. Fixed.", "Parenting is a lot…", "Until it wasn't") lose the 80% who never read on. Localize with "Miami-Dade & Broward" (HCATS #30).
4. Every offer needs a real proposition and a true reason why, plus a lead-by-the-hand close.
B3 "Black Friday" has no deal, and AD 02's only deal breaks the rules (BL Ch. 21). Spell out the step: text your address → get your water system's EWG report → free 14-contaminant test → one flat written quote (BL Ch. 16). Never invent deadlines or bonuses. Fill B3 only with an approved, non-price offer and a real end date.
5. Lead with real proof: real reviews (with consent), honest demos, plain-English stats.
The two highest-scoring ads are the two real Google reviews (A2, B2). Get Sofia R.'s and David M.'s written OK. Replace actor "testimonials" and the sludge demo with real test readings (HCATS #12–14). Say "a forever chemical" before "PFHxS", and keep the EWG-vs-federal caveat.
BW rules used for every rewrite: no price; financing = $0 down, 0% interest, payments as low as $69/mo; social proof = 1000+ customers, 5 stars; warranty = extended 15-year warranty (never lifetime); claims only from biscaynewater.com or the EWG sheet facts (always with the caveat); no fake testimonials. Site mismatches to fix before ads run: the live site still says 10-year tank & valve warranty, "100+ customers" and "75+ Google reviews." Brackets [ ] = a fact you have to supply (real SMS number, approved Black Friday bonus, real test readings).
*EWG Tap Water Database, Miami Dade Water and Sewer Authority (PWS FL4130871), checked Nov 22, 2026. EWG health guidelines are stricter than federal limits; Miami's water meets federal limits. Your home may differ.

Golden principles: Gary Halbert

  1. Market first: find the starving crowd. "The only advantage I want… is A STARVING CROWD!" Pick people who have shown hunger for the result before writing a word. Find the market first, then the product. (BL Ch. 6; BL Ch. 22; GHL "The One Advantage", Oct 1986)
  2. Get into the A-pile. People read what looks personal and skip what looks like an ad. "You can do a better selling job when at first it does not appear you are attempting to do a sales job." Write like a person, use editorial or native formats, no "teaser" hype. (BL Ch. 11; BL Ch. 18)
  3. AIDA, in order. Attention, Interest, Desire, Action. Write each part on purpose. (BL Ch. 15–16; GHL "killer headlines")
  4. Attention must be relevant and honest. A grabber has to "tie in naturally" with the message. A bait headline like "A Submarine That Flies?" is "a cheap shot. People resent this kind of fraud." (BL Ch. 14; BL Ch. 16; GHL "grabbers")
  5. Headlines: news plus a benefit, specific. The strongest headlines put news in, promise a benefit, or both. Specifics make them believable ("In Just 17-Days"). Re-headlining an ad once lifted its pull 475%. Customize to the reader ("How To Buy L.A. Real Estate…"). (GHL "killer headlines"; BL Ch. 9–10)
  6. Interest comes from facts and story. Feed the reader interesting facts and tell a story. A message that "doesn't tell an interesting story" is the most common thing missing. (BL Ch. 16; GHL "AIDA"; GHL "interesting facts")
  7. Desire comes from benefits pictured in the reader's life. Turn facts into benefits ("a fact is… a benefit is what your product will do for the buyer"). Use word pictures. "Always do even the obvious." (BL Ch. 16–17; GHL "best copywriter")
  8. Believability comes from exact details. "Instead of 'most car owners' write '77.6% of all car owners'." Small concrete details make it real. (BL Ch. 23)
  9. The proposition and the reason why. The deal is "the heart and soul of great copywriting." Every good deal needs a believable reason, and the strongest reason is "you, by virtue of some unique circumstance, are special." (BL Ch. 21; GHL "best copywriter")
  10. Close hard and clear. Lead them by the hand: say exactly what to do, how, and why now. Say what they get if they act and what they lose if they wait. Up to 25% of an ad can be the close. (BL Ch. 16)
  11. Plain, flowing, easy to read. Simple words ("get," not "procure"), short sentences and paragraphs, transitions, questions you answer yourself, eye relief. "The very best writing goes unnoticed." "If you are writing for applause… you will go home with empty pockets!" (BL Ch. 17–18)
  12. The decision happens at first glance. The sale is "largely made the instant a prospect sees your ad and reads your headline." The reader then looks for reasons to believe it. (BL Ch. 19)
  13. Prep before you write. Build a Confidential Fact Sheet, then a Benefit List, then a Deal Sheet. Keep a swipe file. Hand-copy proven ads, read copy out loud, and rewrite every place you stumble. (GHL "best copywriter"; BL Ch. 10, 15, 17, 22, 23)
  14. Test small, then roll out. Test 1,000–5,000, analyze, scale the winners. (BL Ch. 22; GHL "aakh improve response")

Golden principles: David Ogilvy

  1. "We sell, or else." You can't bore people into buying. Judge an ad by sales, not awards. "I don't want you to tell me that you find it 'creative.' I want you to find it so interesting that you buy the product." (Conf ch. 1 notes; HCATS #8; OoA ch. 1)
  2. Positioning is the most important decision. Decide what the product is and who it is for before writing. (HCATS #1)
  3. Large, unique promise. "A promise is not a claim, or a theme, or a slogan. It is a benefit." Promise one benefit that is unique and competitive, then go all in on it. (HCATS #2, #11)
  4. Big idea. Ask: did it make me gasp? Do I wish I'd thought of it? Is it unique? Does it fit the strategy perfectly? Could it run 30 years? (HCATS #4; OoA ch. 2)
  5. Do your homework; make the product the hero. Study the product, the competitors and the customer's own words. "There are no dull products, only dull writers." (OoA ch. 2; HCATS #8)
  6. Headlines carry the ad. About five times as many people read the headline as the body. Put the brand and the promise in it, flag the prospect, localize it ("include the name of the city"), use news, and keep it simple. No puns, blind headlines, or negatives. 6–12 words pull best in mail order; long headlines sell. Quote marks lift recall about 26%. (HCATS #25–31; Conf ch. 6; OoA ch. 7)
  7. Give the facts, not adjectives. "The consumer isn't a moron; she is your wife." Avoid superlatives and "brag and boast." Be specific: percentages, time, measured results. Rolls-Royce: 13+ numbered facts, one 18-word fact headline. (Conf ch. 5–6; OoA ch. 7; the Rolls-Royce ad)
  8. Body copy: one person, plain words, a complete pitch. Write as if answering a friend at dinner. Get straight to the point, no analogies, make the first paragraph grab. "Tell the truth, but make the truth fascinating." Every ad is a complete sale. Long copy works if it's relevant. (Conf ch. 6; OoA ch. 7; HCATS #32)
  9. Credible testimonials; helpful information. Testimonials from real customers or relevant experts make copy believable. Helpful advice hooks about 75% more readers. (Conf ch. 6; HCATS #12; OoA ch. 7)
  10. TV and video: grab, problem-solution, honest demo, on-camera voice. Open with an exciting first moment. Show a problem the viewer recognizes, solve it, and prove it, but "don't cheat." Honest visual demos work. On-camera voice beats voice-over. Slice of life still sells. Avoid "logorrhea." Name the brand early and often, and state the benefit early. (HCATS #13–17, #24; OoA ch. 8)
  11. Pictures and captions sell. Real photos beat drawings. Before-and-after works. Use story appeal. Every photo gets a caption that works as a mini ad (brand plus promise). Use editorial layouts. (HCATS #33–37; OoA ch. 7)
  12. Brand image and good manners. Every ad builds the brand's personality, and an ugly or rude ad suggests a shoddy product. "Never write an advertisement which you wouldn't want your family to read." Don't clown. (HCATS #3, #5; Conf ch. 5)
  13. One simple promise, not a committee. Too many messages in one ad add up to nothing, like "the minutes of a committee." (HCATS #11; Conf notes)
  14. Repeat winners; test promises. Split-run headlines. Run a winner until it wears out. (HCATS #38; OoA ch. 12, 15)

Where they disagree

  • Price: Ogilvy says "always try to include the price" (OoA ch. 7). Brand rules can forbid it, and Biscayne Water's do. Use the approved financing line as the price stand-in.
  • Naming competitors: Ogilvy says don't (OoA ch. 7; it confuses readers and can make the rival the hero). Halbert uses contrast freely. Default: contrast with a category ("rental plans," "a fridge filter"), not a named brand, unless legal approves.
  • Emotion vs. facts: Ogilvy found factual commercials usually beat emotional ones (HCATS #23). Halbert sells on emotion and backs it with exact details. Best of both: an emotional promise proved with exact facts.
  • Looking like an ad: Halbert wants it not to look like selling (A-pile). Ogilvy wants the brand in the headline. Fix: native or editorial format with the brand named in the headline or the first caption.

Scoring rubric (1–10, per lens)

  • Score each lens on its own principles above. Start at 10 and drop to the first band whose description fits.
  • 10: Every checklist item for that lens passes. One clear promise, proof you can check, nothing to cut, nothing invented. You'd hand-copy it into a swipe file.
  • 9: One small miss (e.g., brand not in the headline, or a slightly soft close).
  • 8: Strong promise and proof, and 1–2 misses in craft (headline lacks the benefit, body a bit crowded).
  • 7: Competent and on-strategy, but missing one major element (no reason why, weak or blind headline, or generic proof).
  • 6: Clear but forgettable: generic headline, list of features, no story or desire. Or the body tries to say everything.
  • 5: Several major misses, or any house-rule violation or unsupported claim. Any rule violation caps the score at 5 however good the craft is (Ogilvy: "never write an ad you wouldn't want your family to read"; Halbert: "people resent this kind of fraud").
  • 4: Weak or unclear promise plus a cheap or irrelevant grabber, or no real offer behind an offer-style ad.
  • 3: Hard to tell what's sold or why. Misleading demo, invented urgency, or several rule violations.
  • 1–2: Off-brand, deceptive, or no sale at all.
